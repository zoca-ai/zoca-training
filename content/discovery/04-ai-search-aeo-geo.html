1. What it is

People increasingly don't open Google and scroll a list of links. They ask an assistant — "What's a good place for a balayage near downtown?" — and the assistant answers in a sentence or two, naming a few businesses and linking a few sources. AEO/GEO (Answer Engine Optimization / Generative Engine Optimization — two names for the same thing) is the practice of getting your business to be one of the ones it names and cites.

Discovery does two things here:

  1. Measures it — an in-app audit dashboard shows where the business stands across the major AI engines.
  2. Drives it — the same content, listings, reviews, and unique business facts that power the other pillars are what move AI recommendations.

3. How it works

The audit dashboard

The owner clicks Run Audit. Behind the scenes, Discovery generates about 30 real, user-style questions for the business — the kinds of things a customer would actually ask — and runs them across the AI engines, then reports the results.

Four engines are active today: ChatGPT, Gemini, Google AI Overview, and Perplexity. (A few others are supported behind the scenes but not yet turned on.)

The ~30 questions span the way customers really search — discovering a category, asking about a specific service, describing a problem they want solved, comparing options, "near me" searches, asking about trust and reviews, and asking how to actually visit.

A little history: AEO came first, GEO is the umbrella now

HISTORY

AEO (Answer Engine Optimization) is the predecessor; GEO (Generative Engine Optimization) is the bigger thing today. AEO started ~3 years ago, in the Siri / Google Assistant era — you asked a clear question and got back one definite, precise answer pulled straight from existing content. No synthesis, no recommendations, just a direct answer. Then generative models arrived — ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google's AI Overview — and they don't just fetch one answer; they synthesize, curate, and recommend businesses, in a longer, more conversational, two-way way. GEO is the larger umbrella that now covers all of this, which is why AEO and GEO get used interchangeably even though they're not identical. The future of search is conversational, not a typed keyword — that's the GEO world.

E-E-A-T — how content quality gets judged

GOOD TO KNOW

E-E-A-T is Google's framework for evaluating the quality of content: Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness. It's the simplest, fastest lens for understanding why one piece of content gets used and another doesn't. Google applies it to local SEO rankings and to its AI Overview recommendations — so it influences both classic SEO and AI recommendations. The takeaway for a sales conversation: content that scores well on experience, expertise, authority, and trust is what ranks and gets recommended; thin, generic content doesn't. (It's not a guarantee the other engines use the exact same model, but it's the cleanest way to explain content quality.)

The key distinction: recommendations, not rankings

This is the most important thing to get right, and prospects (and trick interview-style questions) will test you on it:

There is no "ranking" in AI search. You don't sit at "position 3." Either the AI recommends and cites you, or it doesn't. AEO/GEO is about mentions and citations, not a position number.

If a prospect asks "how do I rank on AI?", the correct, credible answer corrects the premise: there's no ranking — it's about being recommended and cited, and here's how we drive that.

SAY IT CLEANLY

The vocabulary maps exactly: rankings = SEO (a 1st/2nd/3rd position in Google's list, where the GBP and the map pack live). Recommendations and suggestions = AEO/GEO (the AI names a few businesses with no positional order). There's no "first place" in an AI answer — it either recommends and cites you, or it doesn't. Use the right word for the right pillar and you'll sound like you actually understand the space.

How Zoca actually drives it

The audit measures; the work that moves the needle is the rest of Discovery, pointed at AI:

  • Q&A-formatted content — blogs where each section is a question and an answer, plus FAQs and service pages. That's the format AI engines pull from when someone asks a specific question.
  • Citations from trusted sources — getting cited matters, and it matters more from higher-trust domains. Reviews act as organic trust signals.
  • High-trust listings — pushing the business onto reputable directories and listing platforms so the AI engines have more trustworthy places to find it.
  • Reviews — both for trust signals and because AI engines factor them in.
  • The Brain — the moat. AEO rewards genuinely unique business facts that no competitor has. You can't fool an AI by rephrasing the same generic content everyone else has ("AI slop"). The Brain is the living profile of the business's real facts — the products it uses, what makes it different, who it serves — that keeps the content unique and grounded, which is exactly what gets a business recommended. This is the durable advantage. (See Brain.)
GOOD TO KNOW

The engines behave differently — Perplexity leans on community sources, Gemini draws from Google's index, ChatGPT uses its own crawler — so scores vary engine to engine, and that's expected, not a bug. Voice assistants (Siri, Alexa, Google) are treated as just another way to ask the same question.

7. FAQ & objection handling

"How does AI SEO actually work?"

OBJECTION

We publish structured, FAQ-rich, business-specific content — service pages and Q&A blogs — that AI engines read and cite, and we strengthen the trust signals they rely on: reviews and listings on reputable sites. Then this audit measures your mentions and citations across the major engines so we can keep improving the inputs. It's measurable, and it compounds.

"How do I rank on AI search?" (this is a trick — correct the premise)

OBJECTION

There's actually no "ranking" in AI search. You're not at position three — either the AI recommends and cites you, or it doesn't. So we optimize for mentions and citations and trust signals, not a position number. That's the whole difference between AI search and old-school SEO.

"How fast will I get recommended?"

OBJECTION

There's no guaranteed timeline — I won't pretend otherwise. The AI engines re-index on their own schedule, usually over weeks to a month or more. What moves it is doing all the best practices consistently, and we measure progress right here so you can see it happening rather than taking it on faith.

"I can just write my own content myself."

OBJECTION

One question corners this every time: "How do you know your content is friendly across all the engines — not just the one platform you wrote it on?" Content written in ChatGPT is optimized for ChatGPT; it isn't automatically read well by Gemini, Perplexity, and Google's AI Overview, which each evaluate content differently. Then push on the front end too: who decides what you write about — what keywords, what demand, what's even relevant to your business? And is it backed by your real context, or is it generic? Without a strategy for what to write, content friendly to every engine, and your actual business facts behind it, you're writing random content and hoping. That's the gap the product fills.

"Isn't this just hype? Does it even matter yet?"

OBJECTION

It's early — I'll be straight with you, this is a frontier and the rules are still gray. But AI search is growing 30–40% a quarter while regular search is flat, and Google itself calls it the biggest change to search in 25 years. The salons that get in now have a first-mover edge before everyone catches on. And either way, the same work helps your regular Google rank too.
