TL;DR

A Google Business Profile (GBP, formerly Google My Business) is the free listing that puts a salon on Google Search and Google Maps — the box with the map pin, star rating, hours, photos, and "Call/Directions/Website" buttons. It is the single highest-leverage free asset in local search: a claimed, verified, fully-filled profile appears far more often and drives more calls and visits than an incomplete or unclaimed one. The biggest ranking lever is the primary category, followed by completeness, accurate hours, services, photos, and reviews. Your owners live and die in the "local pack" (the top 3 map results), and GBP is what feeds it.

1. What a Google Business Profile actually is

When someone searches "balayage near me" or "salon Austin," Google shows a map with three highlighted businesses — the local pack — and a side panel (the "knowledge panel") for each. All of that is powered by the business's Google Business Profile. It is a free Google product, separate from a website, that controls how a business shows up across Google Search and Google Maps.

KEY TERM

Local pack (a.k.a. "3-pack") — the block of three local business results, with a map, that Google shows above the regular blue links for local-intent searches. Ranking in the 3-pack is the whole game for local businesses; most clicks go there, not to page-one website results.

WHY IT MATTERS

The profile is free, owned by the business, and is the most-seen surface a salon has on Google. A searcher can read reviews, see photos, check hours, and tap Call or Directions without ever visiting the website. For a small salon with no ad budget, GBP is the highest-ROI marketing asset they own — and most owners under-use it.

2. Claiming and verification — the non-negotiable first step

A profile can exist before anyone claims it — Google auto-generates listings from public data, and customers can suggest edits. Claiming means proving you own/manage the business so you control the listing. Verification is Google confirming the business is real and that you're authorized.

In 2025, Google most often requires video verification: a single unedited walkthrough showing the storefront/signage, the interior and equipment, and proof you operate there. Other methods (postcard with a mailed code, phone, email) appear depending on the business. Google chooses the method; you usually can't pick. Review can take up to about 5 business days.

HOW IT WORKS

Sign in at the Google Business Profile site → find or create the listing → claim it → complete the verification Google assigns. Until verified, you can't fully edit the profile, and Google trusts it less. Verification is also now required to run Local Services Ads (since November 2024).

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"We have a website, so we don't need a Google profile." Wrong — they are different things. The website is yours; the GBP is what surfaces in Maps and the local pack. An unclaimed or unverified profile can still show stale hours, a wrong phone number, or competitor-suggested edits — and you have no control over it.

3. The fields — and which ones carry ranking weight

Google ranks local results on three pillars: relevance (does this business match the query?), proximity (how close is it to the searcher?), and prominence (how well-known/trusted is it?). The profile fields feed relevance and prominence; proximity you can't change. The single strongest lever you can control is the primary category.

RULE OF THUMB

Get the primary category exactly right first — it's the biggest free lever. Then drive the profile to 100% complete. Most owners stop at name + address + phone and leave the high-impact fields blank.

4. Reviews live on the profile

Customer reviews and star ratings attach directly to the GBP — they're not a separate website plugin. They influence both ranking (review signals are a meaningful chunk of local-pack weight) and conversion (the vast majority of consumers read reviews before choosing a local business, and most trust them like personal recommendations). Owners can and should respond to reviews; responses are public and signal an active, engaged business.

SALON EXAMPLE

A salon with 4.8 stars and 220 reviews, where the owner replies to most, will out-convert a 4.9-star shop with 11 reviews and zero replies — even if both rank similarly. Volume, recency, and responsiveness all matter, not just the average.

5. Freshness signals — Posts, photos, Q&A

Google rewards profiles that look alive. Posts (Updates, Offers, Events) let a salon publish a "20% off color this week" promo right in the profile. Fresh photos of recent work signal activity. The traditional Q&A tab is being phased out — Google is replacing it with an AI-generated "Ask about this place" answer drawn from the profile's own data (hours, attributes, reviews, photos), which makes accurate profile fields matter even more. Messaging lets customers text the business from the profile, and fast responses became a positive signal in late 2025.

WHY IT MATTERS

A static profile that hasn't changed in two years looks abandoned to both Google and customers. A salon posting weekly offers and uploading fresh transformation photos signals an active business — which feeds prominence and gives searchers a reason to tap "Call."

FOR THE AE

Performance data is your demo gold. Pull up a prospect's profile, show them how many people found them last month but how few took action because the profile is thin or has no booking link. Then frame the fix: complete profile + booking + fresh content = more calls and directions, all measurable in this same dashboard. Note Google's numbers are directional, not perfectly precise — sell the trend, not the decimal.

7. Booking and messaging — turning views into appointments

GBP can host a Book button that lets a customer schedule directly from Search/Maps, and messaging that lets them text the business. The 2026 direction of Google's local algorithm favors profiles that make it easy to act — booking and messaging turn passive profile views into appointments without the customer ever leaving Google.

SALON EXAMPLE

A walk-by searcher finds the salon, sees a 4.8 rating and fresh balayage photos, and taps Book — appointment locked in 30 seconds. Without a booking link, that same searcher has to find the phone number, call during business hours, and hope someone answers between clients. The booking button removes that friction.

8. Common owner mistakes (your talking points)

  • Never claimed/verified the profile — so it's running on stale Google-guessed data.
  • Wrong primary category — invisible for the searches that matter most.
  • Inconsistent name/address/phone (NAP) across the web — confuses Google's prominence signal.
  • Keyword-stuffing the business name — risks suspension.
  • Blank service menu, no photos, no posts — looks abandoned.
  • Ignoring reviews — no responses, no review-generation habit.
  • Forgetting special/holiday hours — customers show up to a locked door.
COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"I edited my hours, so why does Google still show the old ones?" Google reflects profile edits on its own delayed schedule — changes can take time to propagate, and some edits go through a review queue or can be overwritten by user-suggested edits. Always set the change early (e.g., holiday hours ahead of time) rather than the morning of.

Key terms

  • GBP / Google Business Profile — the free Google listing controlling how a business appears on Search and Maps. Formerly "Google My Business (GMB)."
  • Local pack / 3-pack — the top three map-backed local results above the regular links.
  • Knowledge panel — the side/expanded panel showing a single business's details.
  • Primary category — the main business type; the strongest controllable ranking factor.
  • Relevance / proximity / prominence — Google's three local ranking pillars.
  • NAP — Name, Address, Phone; must be consistent everywhere online.
  • Verification — Google confirming the business is real and you're authorized (often via video in 2025).
  • Posts — Updates/Offers/Events published on the profile as freshness signals.
  • Performance / Insights — the dashboard of searches, views, calls, directions, and clicks.
