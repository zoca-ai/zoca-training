TL;DR

Local SEO is the practice of getting a physical business to show up when nearby people search for what it offers — like "hair salon near me" or "gel nails downtown." Unlike regular SEO, it's not about ranking nationwide; it's about winning the little map and the three businesses Google shows above the regular results. The single biggest lever is a complete, well-reviewed Google Business Profile.

1. What "local SEO" actually means

SEO stands for Search Engine Optimization — the work of making a business show up higher in search results without paying for ads. Local SEO is the slice of that aimed at businesses people physically visit. A salon owner doesn't need to outrank salons in another state; she needs to beat the three other salons within a few miles of her chair.

KEY TERM

Local SEO — the set of practices that help a brick-and-mortar (or service-area) business appear when people nearby search for the services it offers.

WHY IT MATTERS

Roughly 46% of all Google searches have local intent, and "near me" searches have exploded over the past few years. For a salon, lash studio, or barbershop, the customers who matter are almost all local — so local search is where the buying decisions happen.

2. Local SEO vs. national / organic SEO

A national e-commerce store competes against the whole internet on relevance and link authority. A local salon competes against a handful of neighbors, and location itself becomes a ranking factor. The table below shows the practical differences.

SALON EXAMPLE

Search "balayage near me" on a phone in Austin and you'll see a small map with three salons pinned, each with a star rating, then the usual website links below. Those three pinned spots are the prize — most clicks and calls go there.

3. The "near me" moment and local intent

When someone types or says "nail salon near me," "open now," or "lash extensions downtown," they're not browsing — they're close to booking. Industry research suggests a large share of "near me" searchers visit a business within a day, and "open now near me" queries have grown sharply. Google even drops the literal phrase "near me" sometimes because your phone's location already tells it where "here" is.

HOW IT WORKS

Mobile + location services = Google knows where the searcher is standing. It then ranks nearby businesses that match the query. The closer and more relevant you are, the better your odds of being one of the few shown.

FOR THE AE

When a salon owner says "I don't really need to be online — I get walk-ins," that's exactly the point: the "walk-in" of 2026 is someone standing on the sidewalk searching "salon near me." If she isn't in the local pack, the walk-in goes to the salon that is.

4. How Google ranks local results: relevance, distance, prominence

Google's own Business Profile help documentation states it uses three things to rank local results:

  • Relevance — "how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for." Driven by your business category, the services you list, and the words on your website and profile.
  • Distance — "how far each business is from the customer who's searching." You can't move your shop, but you can make sure your address and service area are correct.
  • Prominence — "how well-known a business is." Heavily influenced by reviews, citations, and overall web presence.
RULE OF THUMB

You mostly control relevance and prominence. You mostly cannot control distance. So a smart local SEO plan pours energy into the two you can move.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"More reviews = always #1." Not quite. A glowing profile still won't beat a closer competitor for a searcher standing right next to that competitor. Distance can override everything when the searcher is on top of a rival. That's why even great salons see their ranking "move" depending on where the searcher is.

5. The local pack and Google Maps

The local pack (also called the "map pack" or "3-pack") is the boxed cluster of usually three businesses, with a map, that Google shows at the top of local-intent searches. The same listings power Google Maps. Both are fed by your Google Business Profile — not your website directly.

KEY TERM

Local pack / map pack — the top-of-page block of (typically three) local businesses shown with a map for location-based searches.

WHY IT MATTERS

The local pack sits above the regular website links and gets a huge share of clicks and calls. For a salon, getting into those three slots is often worth more than ranking #1 in the regular results below.

6. The Google Business Profile is the center of gravity

A Google Business Profile (GBP) is the free listing a business claims and manages — name, address, phone, hours, categories, photos, services, and reviews. Industry surveys (e.g., Whitespark's Local Search Ranking Factors) repeatedly find the GBP is the single largest influence on local rankings. Google's docs say it plainly: "Businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results."

RULE OF THUMB

If a salon does only one local-SEO thing, it should be: claim the Google Business Profile, fill out everything (correct category, services, hours, photos), and keep it accurate.

SALON EXAMPLE

A med-spa that lists itself only as "Spa" will lose "Botox near me" searches to a competitor whose profile names the actual services. Categories and listed services are how Google judges relevance.

7. NAP consistency and citations

NAP stands for Name, Address, Phone number. A citation is any place online where that NAP appears — Yelp, Apple Maps, Facebook, directories, the local chamber site. Google cross-checks these to confirm the business is real and located where it claims.

KEY TERM

NAP consistency — your Name, Address, and Phone number written identically everywhere online. Even "Suite 4" vs "#4" can muddy the signal.

HOW IT WORKS

When dozens of trusted sites all list the same NAP, Google grows confident the business is legitimate and correctly located — supporting both relevance and prominence. Conflicting listings (old address, two phone numbers) create doubt and can suppress rankings.

FOR THE AE

A classic salon pain: she moved suites or changed her booking number, but the old info still lives on twelve directories. That's a fixable, very common problem — and a great wedge for a conversation about getting listings cleaned up.

8. Reviews — for ranking AND for the sale

Reviews do double duty. As a ranking signal, Google states "more reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking," and local-SEO studies have shown review signals carrying significant weight. As a conversion signal, BrightLocal's consumer research has found the vast majority of consumers read reviews for local businesses and are more likely to choose one after reading positive ones.

What matters about reviews:

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"I got 50 great reviews two years ago, I'm set." Recency and velocity matter — a salon with fresh reviews this month often outperforms one whose great reviews are all stale.

9. On-page local signals: service pages, location pages, schema

The Google Business Profile does the heavy lifting, but the website still supports relevance:

  • Service pages — a dedicated page for each major service ("Balayage," "Lash Lifts," "Men's Fades") helps Google match specific searches.
  • Location pages — for multi-location salons, one page per location with its own address and phone.
  • Schema markup — invisible code (e.g., LocalBusiness schema) that spells out name, address, hours, and services in a format search engines read precisely.
HOW IT WORKS

Schema is like labeling boxes in a moving truck: the content is the same, but clear labels let Google understand it faster and more reliably. It doesn't guarantee rankings — it removes ambiguity.

This framing keeps clients calm and focused: you can't promise "#1 on Google" (nobody honestly can, since distance and the algorithm aren't ours to control), but you can promise to maximize the controllable signals — and those are exactly the ones that win local-pack placement over time.

Key terms

  • SEO — Search Engine Optimization; improving where you appear in unpaid search results.
  • Local SEO — SEO focused on getting a nearby-relevant business found by local searchers.
  • Local pack / map pack / 3-pack — the top-of-results block of (usually three) local businesses shown with a map.
  • Google Business Profile (GBP) — the free, claimable Google listing that feeds the local pack and Maps.
  • Relevance / Distance / Prominence — Google's three stated local ranking factors.
  • NAP — Name, Address, Phone number; the core identity data for a business.
  • Citation — any online mention of a business's NAP (directories, social, review sites).
  • NAP consistency — having identical NAP data everywhere online.
  • Schema markup — structured code (e.g., LocalBusiness) that tells search engines exactly what a page's business details are.
  • Review velocity / recency — how steadily and how recently new reviews arrive.
