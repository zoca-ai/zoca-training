TL;DR

For a local salon, online reviews are the single biggest driver of whether a stranger walks in the door. They build trust, lift conversion, and feed Google's local ranking. "Good" isn't just a high star rating — it's a steady stream of recent, authentic reviews with owner responses. The right way to earn them is to ask every customer after their visit and make it effortless. The wrong way — "review gating," where you only route happy clients to public sites and steer unhappy ones away — violates Google policy and can break FTC rules carrying penalties over $50,000 per violation. Teach owners the ethical playbook, not the shortcut.

1. Why reviews matter

For an independent salon, the buying decision happens before the client ever calls. A potential customer searches "balayage near me," sees a row of businesses, and picks based on the stars and what other people said. Reviews are the modern word of mouth — except they're public, searchable, and permanent.

WHY IT MATTERS

Reviews do three jobs at once. They build trust (social proof from real people), they drive conversion (the click-to-booking decision), and they feed discovery (where you rank in local search). A salon with strong reviews wins the customer three different ways from a single asset.

BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey reports that the vast majority of consumers read online reviews before choosing a local business, and a large share read them regularly. Trust in reviews has softened from its mid-2010s peak — but a meaningful portion of consumers still trust online reviews about as much as a personal recommendation from a friend.

SALON EXAMPLE

Two nail salons sit a block apart. One has 240 Google reviews at 4.7 stars with the owner replying to most of them. The other has 11 reviews at 4.9 stars, the newest from 14 months ago. The 4.7-star salon books more new clients — more volume, more recency, and visible owner engagement beat a higher-but-thin average almost every time.

2. Where reviews live

Reviews are scattered across platforms, but they are not equal. For a US salon, Google is the center of gravity — it's where searches start and where the map pack appears. Other platforms matter as supporting evidence and for specific audiences.

RULE OF THUMB

Win Google first. A salon should concentrate effort on a strong, recent, well-responded-to Google profile before spreading thin across five platforms. One great Google presence outperforms five neglected ones.

3. What "good" looks like

A high star average alone is not the goal. Google and consumers both read a richer picture: how many reviews, how recent, how steadily they arrive, whether the owner responds, and whether the rating looks believable.

SignalWhat "good" looks likeWhy it matters
VolumeEnough reviews to feel credible vs. local competitorsThin counts read as "new" or "unproven"
RecencyNewest reviews within the last few weeksMany consumers only weigh recent reviews; Google weights recency heavily
VelocityA steady, ongoing flow rather than one old burstRankings tend to rise with consistent new reviews and slip when the flow stops
Owner responsesReplies on positive and negative reviewsSignals active, operating business; consumers favor businesses that respond
Rating rangeA believable average (a sprinkle of 4-star and even critical reviews)A wall of nothing-but-5-stars can read as fake
KEY TERM

Review velocity — the rate at which new reviews arrive over time. Google's local ranking responds to ongoing engagement, so a slow-but-steady drip of fresh reviews is more valuable than a large pile that all landed two years ago.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"We want a perfect 5.0." A flawless rating with few reviews can actually hurt credibility — shoppers read both positive and negative detail to form their own opinion. A 4.7 with hundreds of reviews and thoughtful owner replies is more persuasive than a brittle 5.0 with twelve.

4. How to ethically generate reviews

The single most effective tactic is simple: ask every customer, right after the visit, and make it one tap. Memory fades and momentum is highest at checkout, so the ask should happen at or just after the appointment.

HOW IT WORKS

A practical salon flow: at checkout or shortly after, the client gets a text (or scans a QR code at the front desk) with a direct link to the salon's Google review page. No login hunting, no searching — the link opens straight to the review box. Asking everyone, consistently, is what builds the steady velocity Google rewards.

Crucially, the ask must go to every customer — not just the ones you expect to be happy. Selectively asking only satisfied clients is the start of "review gating," covered in Section 6.

FOR THE AE

This is the natural place a reputation product earns its keep. Owners know they "should" ask for reviews but rarely do it consistently. The pain you're solving is the consistency gap: automating the post-visit ask for every client, sending it by text/QR, and making the link effortless. Frame it as turning a sporadic, awkward request into a reliable habit — without crossing the legal lines in Section 6.

5. Responding to reviews — positive and negative

Responses are not optional polish; they are a ranking and trust signal. Replying shows Google the business is active and shows shoppers a real owner who cares.

  • Positive reviews: a short, specific, warm thank-you. Mention the service or stylist when natural. Keep it human, not copy-pasted.
  • Negative reviews: respond promptly, stay calm and professional, acknowledge the concern, and move the detailed resolution offline ("we'd love to make this right — please reach out to us at..."). Never argue, never get defensive, and never reveal private client details.
RULE OF THUMB

Respond to every review you reasonably can, ideally within a day or two. Speed and consistency of responses are themselves engagement signals — and a calm, classy reply to a bad review often impresses future readers more than the complaint hurts.

6. The legal and ethics line — gating and suppression

This is the section AEs must get exactly right, because the easy-sounding shortcut is the one that gets owners in trouble.

KEY TERM

Review gating — surveying customers first and then routing only the happy ones to public review sites (Google, Yelp) while steering unhappy ones to a private feedback form instead. It manufactures an artificially rosy public rating by filtering who gets to post publicly.

COMPLIANCE NOTE

Review gating and blanket suppression of negatives are not just frowned upon — they cross real lines.

Google's content policies prohibit "discouraging or prohibiting negative reviews, or selectively soliciting positive reviews from customers." Detected gating can lead to reviews being removed, lost ranking power, or profile suspension.

The FTC's Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (final rule effective October 21, 2024) bans practices including buying or selling fake reviews, certain insider reviews without disclosure, and review suppression — for example, using unfounded legal or physical threats to remove negative reviews, or misrepresenting that the displayed reviews are all/most reviews when negative ones have been suppressed by rating or sentiment. The FTC can seek civil penalties (currently up to $53,088 per violation, the 2025 level held for 2026).

The compliant alternative is simple: ask everyone for a public review, make it easy, and earn improvement by fixing the underlying service — never by filtering who's allowed to speak.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"Just send the unhappy ones to a private form instead of Google." That is gating. It is exactly the selective-solicitation pattern Google bans and edges into the FTC's suppression territory. The honest move is to invite all customers publicly and use private channels in addition to — not instead of — the public ask.

7. Reputation recovery basics

A salon with a thin, stale, or bruised profile isn't doomed — it's a recoverable position, and recovery is a great reason to adopt a system.

  1. Restart velocity. Begin asking every customer again, consistently. Fresh, recent reviews dilute old ones and signal Google the business is active.
  2. Respond to the backlog. Reply professionally to past negative reviews; a thoughtful late reply still helps future readers.
  3. Fix the root cause. If a theme keeps appearing (wait times, a specific service), address the operation — reviews are a mirror, not just a scoreboard.
  4. Stay patient and legitimate. Recovery comes from real, steady, authentic reviews over weeks and months — never from buying reviews or scrubbing negatives, both of which carry FTC exposure.
FOR THE AE

"Recovery" reframes reputation work for owners who feel behind. They don't need a perfect history — they need a reliable, ethical engine going forward. That forward-looking, automated, every-customer system is the product story, and it's also the legally safe one.

Key terms

  • Review velocity — the rate at which new reviews arrive over time; steady recent flow is weighted more than a large stagnant total.
  • Review recency — how recently reviews were written; many consumers and Google's ranking both favor newer reviews.
  • Review gating — selectively routing only satisfied customers to public review sites; prohibited by Google and tied to FTC suppression concerns.
  • Review suppression — preventing, removing, or hiding negative reviews (e.g., via threats or misrepresenting that displayed reviews are complete); restricted under the FTC's 2024 rule.
  • Map pack — the block of local business listings with ratings shown in Google search/maps results; heavily influenced by reviews.
  • Owner response — a public reply from the business to a review; a trust and engagement signal for both shoppers and Google.
