TL;DR

A no-show is a client who simply doesn't turn up. For a salon, every no-show is an empty chair that can't be re-sold. Zoca lets the front desk mark a no-show, charge a no-show fee (or forfeit the deposit), and waive it for a good reason. Pitch it as: "Stop eating the cost of no-shows."

1. What it is

A no-show is when a client fails to arrive for a confirmed appointment and the appointment time passes. The front desk can mark the appointment as a no-show from the booking's detail panel.

A no-show is different from a cancellation:

  • A cancellation is when the client (or salon) explicitly calls off the appointment before the time. It runs the cancellation policy — typically with a charge window (e.g., "charge if cancelled within 24 hours").
  • A no-show is silence — the client neither shows nor cancels. The front desk marks it, and the no-show policy kicks in.

The two no-show outcomes are tracked distinctly: no-show with fee collected and no-show, fee waived.

SAY IT SIMPLY

"If a client cancels, that's the cancellation policy. If they just ghost you, that's a no-show — and the system catches it and protects you."

2. Why it matters (the salon owner's view)

No-shows are one of the biggest silent revenue leaks in a salon. A two-hour color appointment that ghosts is two hours the chair earns nothing — and it's usually too late to fill.

Zoca attacks the problem from three sides:

  • Deterrence up front — deposits and attendance confirmations make clients think twice about ghosting.
  • Recovery after the fact — a no-show fee (or a forfeited deposit) means a ghosted slot still earns something.
  • Re-selling the slot — the moment a no-show is logged, the slot is freed up so someone else can grab the time.
VALUE LINE

"You can't make every client show up. But you can make sure a no-show doesn't cost you the whole appointment."

3. How it works

Marking a no-show. When a client doesn't show up, the front desk marks the appointment as a no-show from the booking's detail panel. A no-show can be undone if it was a mistake.

The fee. The business sets up a no-show policy that clients must accept during booking. The fee is configurable as either a percentage of the service price or a fixed amount (e.g., a flat $50). How it works depends on the payment method:

Card on file: When the client has a card on file, the business can set up a no-show fee (percentage or fixed). If the client no-shows, the business has the control to charge or not charge the fee to the card on file on a case-by-case basis.

Deposits: The deposit can be configured as refundable or non-refundable. In addition to keeping or forfeiting the deposit, a separate no-show fee can also be applied on top — since the client's card is already collected, the fee can be charged to it.

POLICY IS CONFIGURABLE

The fee amounts above are examples — the salon sets these. Cancellation and no-show policies are configured separately and are set at the location level. If the fee is a percentage of the service price, the actual charge naturally varies by service. Present it as: "you decide the fee at your location."

Waiving. The owner stays in control. If a client had a real emergency, staff can waive the fee in one action; if money was already taken, it's refunded, and the client gets a "we understand, fee waived" message.

Disputes. If a client disputes a no-show, the salon can review and resolve it (waive, partially refund, or uphold the charge), and the whole thing is logged.

Attendance confirmation (deterrence). Salons can require clients to confirm they're coming before the appointment. The client taps to confirm; the stylist is notified. Note this is a signal, not a guarantee — it nudges clients and surfaces flakiness early. If a client reschedules, their confirmation resets so they reconfirm the new time.

Notifications around a no-show. The client gets a no-show notice (with the fee, if any), and the stylist gets an alert. If the fee is waived, the client is told.

6. FAQ & objection handling

"My clients won't like being charged for no-shows." You set the policy, and you can waive any fee for a genuine emergency in one tap. Most owners find a clear, fair no-show policy actually reduces no-shows because clients take the booking seriously.

"How does it know someone didn't show? My front desk is busy." The front desk marks a no-show right from the appointment's detail panel — it's one tap. They can also undo it if it was a mistake.

"What's the difference between a cancellation and a no-show?" A cancellation is the client calling it off ahead of time (cancellation policy applies). A no-show is them not showing and not telling you (no-show policy applies). They're handled by separate rules you control.

"Can I charge different no-show fees for different services?" The no-show policy is set at the location level. However, if the fee is configured as a percentage of the service price, the actual amount will naturally vary by service.

"What if I just want a deposit kept, not a separate charge?" That's the most common setup: the deposit they already paid simply converts into the no-show fee.

"A client says it was a mistake / emergency." Waive the fee in one action (refunding if needed), and they get a friendly "fee waived" message. You can also resolve disputes and keep an audit trail.

7. Key terms

  • No-show — client didn't arrive and didn't cancel; marked by the front desk from the booking's detail panel.
  • No-show fee — the charge for a no-show: a percentage of the service price or a fixed amount. Can be charged to a card on file or applied on top of a deposit.
  • Deposit forfeiture — the already-paid deposit becomes the no-show fee (nothing extra to charge).
  • Fee collected vs fee waived — the two no-show outcomes the system tracks.
  • Cancellation window — for cancellations, the hours-before-appointment threshold where a charge applies (separate from no-shows).
  • Attendance confirmation — an optional "confirm you're coming" step that nudges clients and flags flakiness early.
